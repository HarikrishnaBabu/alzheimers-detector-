Reading early signs of Alzheimer's in brain scans

The challenge

By the time symptoms appear, the disease has been active for years

Alzheimer's begins with quiet biological changes — amyloid buildup, shrinking of the hippocampus, disrupted connectivity — long before memory loss is noticeable in daily life. Standard clinical evaluation tends to catch the disease only once damage is already extensive, which narrows the window for early intervention and care planning.

Imaging offers a way to look earlier. The question is whether a model can learn to spot the faint structural signature of early disease in a scan that, to the eye, still looks close to normal.

  • ~10–20 yrs Brain changes linked to Alzheimer's are believed to begin roughly a decade or more before clinical symptoms emerge.
  • MRI / PET Structural MRI shows atrophy patterns; PET can visualize amyloid and tau deposits — together they capture complementary biomarkers.
  • Subtle signal Early-stage volumetric changes are often too small for the eye to reliably catch, but can be learned from large labeled datasets.

How it works

From raw scan to a flagged region

A simplified view of the pipeline a convolutional model follows to turn a 3D scan into a probability and a visual explanation.

Input scan

MRI / PET volume

A 3D brain scan is loaded as a stack of slices, either structural (MRI) or metabolic (PET).

Preprocessing

Normalize & align

Skull-stripping, spatial registration and intensity normalization put every scan on a common frame of reference.

Feature extraction

3D CNN encoder

A convolutional network learns volumetric patterns — atrophy, texture, and shape — across the whole scan.

Attention map

Localize the signal

The model highlights which regions — often the hippocampus and entorhinal cortex — most influenced its decision.

Output

Class probabilities

A likelihood is produced across cognitively normal, mild cognitive impairment, and Alzheimer's-consistent patterns.

Disease progression

What imaging tends to show at each stage

A general picture of how structural change is thought to progress — not a timeline any individual is guaranteed to follow.

Stage 1

Cognitively normal

No detectable atrophy. Hippocampal volume and connectivity fall within typical ranges for age.

Stage 2

Preclinical

Biomarker changes (amyloid, early volume loss) may be present with no noticeable symptoms yet.

Stage 3

Mild cognitive impairment

Measurable hippocampal shrinkage; mild memory lapses may become noticeable to the person or family.

Stage 4

Mild Alzheimer's

Clearer atrophy and ventricle enlargement; memory and planning difficulties affect daily tasks.

Stage 5

Moderate–severe

Widespread cortical thinning and substantial ventricle expansion accompany significant functional decline.

Neurally is a research and education prototype illustrating how imaging-based AI models are studied for early Alzheimer's detection. It does not provide medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment, and none of the imagery here comes from real patients.
